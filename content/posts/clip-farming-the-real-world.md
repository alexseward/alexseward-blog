When Streamer University returned this summer, a tidal wave of clips across X and Reddit made sure I knew about it. Against my will, I learned that 120 creators had been recast as students on a real college campus in Arkansas, joined by established streamers playing professors, and given several days to broadcast themselves learning how to broadcast themselves. On orientation day, Kai Cenat sat behind a dean's desk with a [command centre showing the other streams](https://www.complex.com/pop-culture/a/jaelaniturnerwilliams/kai-cenat-streamer-university-2026-monitoring-setup), checking whether everyone was in class.

It ought to have fascinated me. Here was a generation inventing its own entertainment industry, building careers outside traditional media and teaching one another how it worked. Instead, everything I saw felt fake and staggeringly boring. The event is over, but I am still trying to work out what so many people saw in it.

Streaming sells access as authenticity. If somebody is live for eight hours, surely we must be seeing their real self. Yet a personality rewarded day after day for louder reactions, conflict and quotable moments can harden into a character without ever being scripted. The audience may believe it is watching somebody being themselves when it is really watching the version of them that survived the algorithm.

The boundary may not be clear to the person performing it either. Streamer University assembled a temporary world in which these characters could perform living together, and every lesson, friendship, argument and corridor encounter carried the knowledge that it might become a clip.

There is already a term for deliberately staged or exaggerated moments designed to escape a stream and circulate elsewhere: [clip farming](https://www.vulture.com/article/kai-cenat-streamer-university-what-happened.html). What interests me is what happens when that logic extends beyond a fake argument or prank and into the shape of the day itself—when you begin clip farming the real world.

The audience was not simply following a programme. It could move between dozens of people simultaneously producing their own versions of the same event, waiting for something to happen. Across the six-day event, coverage from around 6,000 channels generated an estimated [56.79 million hours watched](https://streamscharts.com/news/streamer-university-2026-results). That is almost 6,500 years of collective human attention compressed into less than a week.

I cannot stop thinking about the weight of that number. Not because young people should be using every spare hour productively; wasting time together is one of the pleasures of being young. It is the industrial scale of the waiting that unsettles me. An enormous amount of human life can now be gathered around almost nothing happening, on the promise that something eventually might.

I am aware that this makes me sound old. Specifically millennial old: able to understand every piece of the technology and still feel completely lost when those pieces are assembled into a culture.

Celebrity has always confused me. Reality television made it stranger by turning being watched into a talent of its own. Influencers made the relationship more direct, as though celebrity could be reverse-engineered through enough access and repetition. Live streamers have taken the idea to its logical conclusion: you can now watch somebody perform a version of their life for eight hours at a time.

More than anything, I do not understand the humour. Many of the most famous streamers seem almost completely uninteresting and unfunny to me. They shout, repeat themselves, react dramatically and talk in references that appear to become funny through circulation rather than construction. Streamer University intensified all of this by putting them in the same place, each trying to create moments from people who were also trying to create moments.

## Reality television without the television

This did not begin with Twitch. Radio phone-ins, early webcams and the live feeds from *Big Brother* all offered versions of continuous access. In 2007, Justin Kan took the idea literally. He wore a [head-mounted camera connected to batteries and mobile data cards](https://techcrunch.com/2007/03/19/kiko-guys-back-as-reality-tv-stars/) and broadcast his life on Justin.tv, twenty-four hours a day.

Gaming became the part of Justin.tv that people really wanted to watch, and in 2011 its gaming-focused offshoot launched as Twitch. Streaming appeared to have found its natural subject. Games already contained objectives, conflict, skill, jeopardy and a steady supply of moments worth reacting to. The streamer added personality; the audience added company.

Now the camera has moved back outside.

IRL streaming looks like an evolution away from gaming, but it may be better understood as a return to lifecasting with the grammar of video games still attached.

The chat becomes a heads-up display. Donations and viewer counts provide the score. A new city is another map, a stranger a potential non-player character, and an awkward encounter a possible side quest. The streamer's life supplies an open world in which something entertaining could happen at any moment.

This is where the eight-hour length starts to make more sense. A conventional programme has to earn each minute. A live stream sells the possibility that the next minute will be different. Most of it can be uneventful because boredom is the runway for the clip everyone will share later.

The humour may work the same way. I am listening for a joke, while the audience is participating in a shared language. The funny part is not always what the streamer says. It might be the reaction in chat, a reference to an older stream, or the feeling of having watched a moment turn into a meme in real time.

## A life designed to be clipped

Reality television was always manufactured, despite the name. Producers selected the cast, created situations and compressed days of footage into a story. Streaming presents itself as the correction: no edit, no script, no distance between the person and the audience.

But longer does not necessarily mean more authentic.

Reality television edits ordinary life into drama after it happens. Streaming can reorganise life in advance so that something clip-worthy is always likely to happen. Clip farming, at its broadest, is not merely staging a fake argument. It is arranging real life around the possibility that any moment might become content.

Streamer University made that reversal unusually visible. It was presented partly as a genuine opportunity for emerging creators to learn from successful ones, and that opportunity may have been valuable. Yet the lessons could not sit outside the entertainment. The university itself had to keep producing content, so education became the premise of a multi-channel reality show.

The day needs characters, movement, conflict and surprise. Silence becomes dead air. Reflection is risky because the viewer can leave at any second. Other people enter the frame not necessarily as people with lives of their own, but as possible content. A restaurant, football match or foreign city becomes a level through which the streamer moves, searching for the next interaction.

This is the part I find most uncomfortable. Public life becomes a film set whose extras were never asked to take part. Research into [how live streamers manage bystanders' privacy](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/do-streamers-care-about-bystanders-privacy-an-examination-of-live/) found that current streaming platforms lacked features to help streamers seamlessly manage bystander privacy or involve the people being filmed in privacy decisions. The stranger in the background may be a person to everyone else, but to the stream they are scenery until something makes them useful.

Even the streamer is not entirely in control. Chat requests a reaction. Donations reward a behaviour. Clippers wait for the few seconds that can escape into TikTok, X or YouTube Shorts.

The person who appears to be playing the world like a game is also being played by the audience and the platform; the main character is simultaneously an avatar.

## The bystander becomes a broadcaster

I keep thinking about the crowd in *Black Mirror*'s *White Bear*. The image was horrifying because ordinary people had stopped behaving like witnesses. A woman ran past them in terror and they simply held up their phones.

What now feels strange is not that the episode imagined everyone filming (although this was still relatively novel in 2013!), but how quickly filming became unremarkable, then live, then a plausible way to make a living.

The phone is no longer just a camera. For an IRL streamer, protest broadcaster or online auditor, it is the camera crew, studio, distribution network, payment terminal and employer. The person holding it is no longer merely a spectator.

They are at work.

That changes the relationship between the camera and the event. People are not always filming because something happened. They may go somewhere because something *might* happen, or because the presence of the camera itself makes a confrontation more likely. A protest, police station, council office, shop or vulnerable stranger becomes a possible episode.

The progression seems to have been:

*I happened to capture this.*

Then:

*I show people what is happening.*

Then:

*People follow me because I show what is happening.*

And finally:

*I need something to happen because people follow me.*

That final step is where the camera stops documenting reality and starts exerting pressure on it. A calm interaction is commercially useless. Irritation, conflict and humiliation are valuable. The audience does not simply watch the result afterwards; through comments, donations and algorithmic rewards, it can encourage escalation in real time.

*White Bear* was shocking because ordinary people treated reality like television.

Now ordinary people are trying to turn reality into their television career.

## The room has a checkout

TikTok Shop, eBay Live and Whatnot feel like the most vulgar expression of all this technology and culture coming together. The intimacy of the streamer, the participation of chat, the urgency of a game and the machinery of online shopping fit inside the same screen.

The room still forms around the personality, but now the room has a checkout.

[TikTok Shop puts products inside videos and live streams](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/introducing-tiktok-shop?lang=en), with creators able to earn commission on sales. [eBay Live combines demonstrations and chat with auctions](https://www.ebay.com/help/buying/ebay-live/livestream-shopping-ebay-live?id=5421) that usually last under two minutes. On Whatnot, many auctions [finish in thirty seconds or less](https://help.whatnot.com/hc/en-us/articles/14932924544141-Bid-on-an-item-during-a-show); its Sudden Death format adds no extra time for late bids, and the winner's saved payment method is charged automatically.

Television shopping already knew how to turn a presenter, a product demonstration and a sense of urgency into entertainment. What feels different is how neatly that older sales pitch fits the culture streaming has built. The host can respond to you, other viewers supply the atmosphere, and bidding gives you something to do inside the show. Buying can become a way of joining in, competing or being noticed by somebody you have spent hours watching.

I can see why collectors might enjoy this. A knowledgeable seller showing an object live and answering questions can offer something a static listing cannot. A room full of people who care about the same obscure things can be good company.

Those pleasures also give the sales pitch somewhere to take hold.

Affection for a host and excitement in chat can become difficult to separate from wanting the object on screen.

That is what I find vulgar about it. So much ingenuity has gone into making distant people feel present to one another, and here that feeling is organised around getting them to spend. The countdown gives hesitation a cost. The auction turns spending more into winning. Even the viewer who buys nothing helps populate the room in which somebody else is being sold to.

Earlier, I wondered whether I was missing the belonging inside all the noise. Live shopping makes me wonder what happens once that belonging becomes part of the sales technique.

We have built the means for almost anyone to broadcast to the world, and one of the uses taking shape is an endless shopping channel where the audience helps sell the stock.

## The Bourdain test

IShowSpeed is the person who complicates my easy dismissal of all this.

His early appeal was built around the loud, exaggerated reactions that still dominate his persona, but what he now produces is genuinely ambitious. The camera has left the bedroom. His streams have become live travel programmes with crews, logistics and production quality that would have been impossible for an individual broadcaster a decade ago.

His Africa tour crossed twenty countries in twenty-eight days, with streams [lasting as long as nine hours](https://apnews.com/article/ishowspeed-stream-africa-tour-7c4083f24e3e2cb2c739154283faa1a4). It showed an enormous young audience places and cultures that conventional American media often reduces to a narrow set of images. By the World Cup he had become so embedded in football culture that he [opened FIFA's closing ceremony by performing his song *Champions*](https://www.fifa.com/en/tournaments/mens/worldcup/canadamexicousa2026/articles/best-of-closing-ceremony-halftime-show). Afterwards, he marvelled that only four years earlier he had been streaming from his mother's house, barking at the camera and lighting things on fire.

I completely understand the appeal of IShowSpeed. I can see the evolution of both the person and the producer, and the scale of what he is trying to build.

I just wish he were a bit more insightful.

Anthony Bourdain is probably an unfair comparison. His programmes were researched, edited and supported by experienced production teams. He was also in his forties when *No Reservations* began, while IShowSpeed is twenty-one and broadcasting live to an audience that expects constant interaction. They are products of different media, generations and incentives.

Even so, Bourdain represents something I want from travel broadcasting. His charisma directed attention away from himself. Food opened into history, politics, work and the daily lives of other people. He could be funny, abrasive and theatrical, but curiosity was the point. He made himself smaller so that a place could become larger.

IRL streaming often does the reverse. A place is pulled into the orbit of the streamer's existing character. The food becomes a reaction, the stranger becomes an encounter and the culture becomes a backdrop. We learn what happened to the streamer in a country, but not always much about the country itself.

That criticism is not entirely fair to IShowSpeed either. A [*Wired* profile described him as a "global group chat"](https://www.wired.com/story/big-interview-ishowspeed-world-cup/), which gets closer to what the form is trying to achieve. His viewers are not asking for a documentary. They want to experience the journey with him, influence it and watch somebody familiar collide with somewhere unfamiliar.

His lack of distance may be precisely the appeal.

The question is whether proximity has to come at the expense of insight. The production and ambition have evolved. Perhaps the persona can too—not into a solemn documentarian, but into somebody whose reactions begin with genuine attention to what is in front of him.

## Go for the content

A few years ago I wrote about the difference between spectators and contributors at live events. The best football crowds do not simply consume an atmosphere; they create it. I called the post *Go for the Culture*.

The World Cup streams made me think about its inverse.

I kept seeing streamers broadcasting from inside World Cup stadiums, at matches where [seats were changing hands for thousands of dollars](https://apnews.com/article/1eb6731d9cb7dc1bde02b41c9992174c), but looking towards a phone and talking to chat. The match, crowd and stadium had become the background to a parallel event for an audience elsewhere.

A seat inside the culture had become a studio for content.

That sounds more damning than I intend. Streaming can also bring millions of people into places they could never afford to visit. It can translate, improvise and respond in ways television cannot. IShowSpeed's audience is not passively receiving a finished story; it helps steer a live one.

There is something genuinely new and powerful in that.

Perhaps this is simply the awkward adolescence of a medium. Early reality television did not lead only to *Love Island*. YouTube did not remain a collection of webcam monologues. Streaming may be developing the infrastructure for a kind of broadcasting we have not properly seen yet: live, global, participatory and still capable of paying attention.

The next Anthony Bourdain may not make television. They may travel with a phone, a satellite connection and millions of people talking in their ear.

I only hope that, occasionally, they stop talking to chat long enough to notice where they are.
